# Markets and Assets

## Choosing a market

"Market" here means the geography, sector, or segment the research is
focused on, before picking specific vehicles inside it.

Considerations to walk through:
- **Home bias vs. diversification** — most people naturally hold too much in
  their home market simply because it's familiar. That's not wrong on its
  own, but worth naming explicitly so it's a choice, not a default.
- **Developed vs. emerging markets** — developed markets are typically lower
  volatility with more mature regulation; emerging markets can offer higher
  growth potential with higher volatility and often weaker regulatory/legal
  protection for investors.
- **Broad market vs. sector-concentrated** — a broad market (or global) fund
  spreads risk across many companies and industries; a sector bet (e.g. only
  technology, only energy) concentrates both the upside and the downside.
  Concentrated sector bets should generally be sized as a smaller portion of
  a new investor's plan, if included at all.
- **Currency risk** — if the user's everyday spending currency differs from
  the market they're investing in, currency movements can add or subtract
  from returns independent of how the underlying assets perform. Worth
  naming even if the user chooses not to hedge it.

## Choosing an asset (vehicle)

Common categories to walk through, matched against the risk profile and
horizon already established:

| Asset type | Typical role | Notes for a new starter |
|---|---|---|
| Cash / cash equivalents | Capital preservation, short-term goals, emergency fund | Lowest volatility, but returns may not keep pace with inflation |
| Bonds / fixed income | Income, capital preservation, dampening portfolio volatility | Generally lower volatility than equities; sensitive to interest rate changes |
| Individual stocks | Growth, direct ownership | Highest single-security risk — one company's problems can sharply hurt this holding; requires ongoing research per company held |
| Index funds / ETFs | Growth with built-in diversification | Spreads risk across many holdings in one purchase; usually lower fees than actively managed funds; a common default starting point for new investors precisely because it lowers both research burden and concentration risk |
| Actively managed mutual funds | Growth, professional selection | Higher fees than index funds; performance depends on the manager, and most active managers underperform their benchmark index over long periods after fees — worth naming as context, not a blanket rule against all active funds |
| Real estate (direct or REITs) | Income, diversification, inflation hedge | Direct property is illiquid and capital-intensive; REITs offer real-estate exposure with stock-like liquidity |
| Commodities | Diversification, inflation hedge | Can be volatile and don't generate income on their own (no dividends/interest) |
| Cryptocurrency | Speculative growth | Historically very high volatility and a less mature regulatory environment than traditional assets — flag clearly as higher-risk and size accordingly if the user wants exposure at all |

For a genuinely new starter with no strong asset-picking expertise yet, it's
reasonable to point out that a broad, low-cost index fund/ETF is a common
default starting point precisely because it solves market choice,
diversification, and reduces the research burden in one purchase — while
still making clear that the final call is theirs and depends on the goal,
budget, and risk profile already established.
