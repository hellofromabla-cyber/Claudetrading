# Broker Checklist

"Broker" here just means the trading app or bank platform the user actually
opens an account with and clicks "buy" through — Robinhood-style apps,
online banks' investing arms, traditional brokerages, etc. Pick it last,
once the plan (goal, budget, risk profile, timing, market, asset,
diversification, strategy) is defined — otherwise the platform's
limitations end up quietly shaping the plan instead of serving it (e.g.
picking assets only because "that's what this app offers").

**Never assert that a specific broker is "the best" or "regulated" from
memory** — regulatory status, fee schedules, and available markets change,
and getting this wrong could put the user's money somewhere unsafe. If you
have live web search available, use it to find current, sourced options.
If not, hand the user this checklist and point them to primary sources
(the relevant financial regulator's public register is the authoritative
way to verify a broker is actually licensed — not the broker's own
marketing site).

## The deciding factor: fees

Once a shortlist of candidate apps/banks all pass the gate checks below,
**fees are usually the single biggest lever a new starter actually
controls** — unlike market returns, fees are known in advance, are paid
regardless of how the investment performs, and compound against the
account over decades. Compare candidates on the full fee picture, not just
the headline number an app advertises (many lead with "$0 commission" while
making it back elsewhere):

- Trading/commission fees per transaction
- Account or platform maintenance fees
- Foreign exchange (FX) conversion fees, if investing in a different
  currency than the account's base currency
- Fund-level fees (expense ratios) on top of the app/bank's own fees —
  matters even on a "free" trading app if the funds available on it carry
  high expense ratios
- Inactivity fees
- Withdrawal or account-closure fees

A small percentage-point difference in ongoing fees (e.g. platform fee or
fund expense ratio) sounds trivial but compounds significantly over a long
horizon — worth saying explicitly, since it's easy for a new starter to
fixate on the per-trade commission and miss the ongoing fee that matters
more for a buy-and-hold plan.

## Gate checks (must pass, but not what should decide between finalists)

- **Regulatory status** — is the app/bank licensed/regulated by a recognized
  financial authority in the user's jurisdiction? Check the regulator's own
  public register, not just a badge on the platform's website. This is a
  pass/fail safety check, not a point of comparison between two regulated
  options.
- **Investor protection scheme** — is there a deposit/investor compensation
  scheme covering the account if the platform fails, and what's the coverage
  limit?
- **Market and asset access** — does it actually offer the market(s) and
  asset type(s) chosen in the earlier steps (e.g. international stocks,
  specific fund providers, fractional shares if the budget is small)? If it
  doesn't, it's not a candidate regardless of how low its fees are.
- **Account minimums** — does it fit the budget decided earlier, including
  minimums for regular/automatic contributions if that's part of the plan?
- **Tax-advantaged account options** — many jurisdictions offer
  tax-advantaged wrappers for retirement or general investing (the specific
  names and rules vary by country); check whether the platform supports the
  ones relevant to the user's jurisdiction and goal — this can outweigh a
  small fee difference on its own.
- **Usability and tools** — does the interface fit how the user plans to
  interact with it (e.g. automatic recurring investment support, if DCA is
  part of the strategy)?
- **Customer support and reputation** — responsiveness, and independent
  reviews rather than only the platform's own testimonials.

## Red flags

- Guarantees of high or "risk-free" returns
- Pressure to deposit quickly or trade frequently
- No verifiable regulatory registration
- Unusually difficult or fee-laden withdrawal process
