# Risk Profile

Two distinct dimensions get conflated as "risk tolerance." Both matter, and
they can point in different directions (e.g. someone with high risk
*capacity* but low risk *tolerance* — plenty of years to recover from a loss,
but who knows they'd panic-sell in a 30% drop).

## Risk capacity (financial — what they can afford)

Questions that surface this:
- How many years until the money is needed? (longer = more time to recover
  from a downturn)
- Is this the only pot of savings, or one of several? (more diversification
  across pots = more capacity in any one of them)
- Is income stable, or could it stop unexpectedly? (job security affects
  how much of a portfolio loss can be absorbed without disrupting life)
- Is there other capital or an emergency fund to fall back on if this
  investment drops sharply right when it's needed?

## Risk tolerance (emotional — what they can stomach)

Questions that surface this:
- "If this dropped 30% in a month, what would you do?" — a surprisingly
  reliable gut-check question. "Sell everything" vs. "wait it out" vs. "buy
  more" reveals a lot.
- Have they experienced a market downturn before (2008, 2020, a personal
  investment loss)? What did they actually do at the time, not what they
  wish they'd done?
- Would they check their portfolio daily, or are they comfortable not
  looking for months at a time? Frequent checking during volatility tends
  to increase anxiety and the odds of an emotional decision.

## Reconciling the two

When capacity and tolerance disagree, **tolerance is usually the binding
constraint** for a new investor — a technically-optimal-but-terrifying
portfolio that gets abandoned in the first real downturn produces a worse
outcome than a more conservative one the person actually sticks with. It's
fine, and often correct, to recommend starting a notch more conservative
than pure capacity would suggest, especially before someone has lived
through a real downturn with real money in the market.

Avoid reducing this to a single number or label (e.g. "you're a 7/10") —
that's the kind of quantitative output a licensed advisor's questionnaire is
built and validated to produce. Instead, describe the profile in plain terms
("high capacity, but untested tolerance — worth starting conservative and
increasing equity exposure as you get comfortable riding out volatility")
and let it inform the diversification plan and strategy rather than
producing a false-precision score.
